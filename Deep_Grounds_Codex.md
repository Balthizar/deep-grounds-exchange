# THE DEEP GROUNDS CODEX
### A Complete Sourcing and Design Doctrine for Adventurers League Remasters

*This document is self-contained. It assumes no prior familiarity with Adventurers League policy, tournament roleplaying history, or the West Marches campaign style. Everything needed to understand and apply this doctrine is explained here from first principles. Treat it as a core rulebook, not a set of notes referencing other books.*

---

## FRONT MATTER — WHAT THIS DOCUMENT IS

This is the design doctrine behind every remaster produced for the Deep Grounds Adventuring Company table — an ongoing project converting official Adventurers League (AL) modules into fully standalone, print-ready documents run at a single physical table under a persistent in-fiction frame.

Every design decision in a remaster — how an encounter is scaled, how an NPC is voiced, how a puzzle is structured, how a rumor is planted — is resolved by consulting four layers of authority, in strict descending order. This document explains what each layer is, why it exists, exactly what it says, and how the layers interact. It closes with the practical procedure used to apply all of it to an actual module.

---

## CHAPTER 1 — THE COMPLIANCE HIERARCHY

### 1.1 What Adventurers League Is

D&D Adventurers League (AL) is Wizards of the Coast's official organized-play program. It exists so that a Dungeon Master and a table of strangers — people who have never played together before and may never play together again — can sit down with an official adventure and have a legally consistent, fair experience, regardless of which store, convention, or living room they're playing in. That consistency requirement is the entire reason AL's rules are written the way they are: as binding policy, not suggestions.

AL publishes its policy across three official documents, plus the text of the adventure itself. These four things do not carry equal weight. They are ranked, and the ranking is explicit in AL's own published guidance, not something inferred by individual tables.

### 1.2 The Four Tiers of AL Authority

**Tier 1 — The Adaptation Guide (ALAG).**
A supplementary document that provides adventure-specific corrections for select modules — usually older adventures that need updating to work under current rules, or officially-licensed content (like tie-ins to a streamed campaign) that needs special handling. Not every adventure has ALAG guidance. When it exists, it overrides everything else for that specific point.

**Tier 2 — The Dungeon Master's Guide (ALDMG).**
The rulebook written specifically for AL Dungeon Masters. It covers how to run a table fairly, how to adjust encounters within strict limits, what optional rules are permitted, safety-tool expectations, and general session-structure guidance (downtime awards, leveling options, etc.). Where ALAG is silent, this document governs.

**Tier 3 — The Player's Guide (ALPG).**
The rulebook written for players, covering character creation, advancement, magic item handling, and what happens to a character between sessions. Where ALAG and ALDMG are both silent on a point, this document governs.

**Tier 4 — The Adventure Text Itself.**
The actual printed module — the one being remastered. Its instructions are followed only where none of the three tiers above it say anything different.

### 1.3 Why the Order Matters

This isn't a matter of preference. AL's own Adaptation Guide states plainly that its own guidance overrides the adventure's text and the general guidance in the other two guides, and that where no Adaptation Guide exists, the DM's Guide is followed first, then the Player's Guide, then the adventure. The DM's Guide restates the identical order from the Dungeon Master's side of the table.

The practical consequence: **there is no "spirit of the rules" clause anywhere in this tier.** A DM cannot look at a printed rule, decide a different approach would produce a better table experience, and simply run it that way while still calling the result AL-legal. Compliance here means literal, textual compliance — not judgment calls about what "should" be more fun. That judgment call is reserved for the layers below this hierarchy, never for this one.

### 1.4 What Belongs in Tier 1–4 Territory

Anything touching character legality, magic item availability, XP and leveling mechanics, encounter-difficulty math, downtime rules, or anything else that determines whether a character or a session counts as "legal" AL play belongs here. If AL's own documents speak to it, that answer is final for purposes of this project, and no lower layer may contradict it.

## CHAPTER 2 — TOURNAMENT CRAFT (THE RPGA LAYER)

### 2.1 What the RPGA Was

Long before Adventurers League existed, TSR (the original publisher of Dungeons & Dragons, decades before Wizards of the Coast acquired the game) ran an official organized-play network called the RPGA — the Role Playing Game Association. Its flagship product for teaching people how to design and run competitive tournament-style adventures was *The Official RPGA Tournament Handbook*, published in 1987. That handbook is the foundation of this chapter.

Where the AL hierarchy in Chapter 1 governs *legal compliance*, RPGA doctrine governs something AL's own documents mostly don't touch at all: **the craft of designing and running a genuinely good table.** This layer is consulted whenever AL is silent on a design question, and it is applied by default — not merely considered — wherever it actually fits a modern table (some of it is tournament-scoring-specific and doesn't translate; judgment is still required).

### 2.2 The Five Things a Good Table Actually Tests

The RPGA handbook holds that a good session tests five distinct player skills, not one:

1. **Rules knowledge** — understanding the system well enough to use it.
2. **Teamwork and cooperation** — functioning as a group, not five soloists.
3. **Problem-solving and innovation** — finding solutions the designer didn't spell out.
4. **Roleplaying** — inhabiting a character convincingly across different situations.
5. **Strategic response to combat** — tactical thinking when the dice start flying.

A table that only ever tests combat tactics is running a thinner game than one that gives all five room to matter. This is the earliest documented version of a principle AL's own current DM's Guide restates in a different form: that a DM should adjudicate fairly, without inventing standing house-rules, and should let the system's actual mechanics — not the DM's improvisation — carry the weight of a ruling.

### 2.3 The Fourteen Design Goals

The handbook lays out fourteen numbered principles for building a good adventure. Restated here in full, because they form the actual backbone of encounter design at this table:

1. **Fun for every participant is the only real success metric.** A tournament (or, for this project, a session) is not measuring who can be eliminated fastest. Challenge should stretch players, not punish them out of the game.
2. **Live up to the genre's own ideals.** Heroic fantasy should deliver humor, noble stakes, and spectacular moments. Content that is merely destructive or anti-heroic works against the genre, not with it.
3. **No player should ever become an audience member.** This principle explicitly warns against: a high chance of character death in the opening encounters, encounters only one or two characters can meaningfully engage with, slow-paced openings, and puzzles or plots that hinge on a single specific character succeeding.
4. **Never manufacture conflict between player characters.** If the adventure needs a traitor or a spy working against the party, that role belongs to an NPC — never a PC.
5. **Open at a dramatic or exciting point**, so the table has momentum from the first minute.
6. **Every round needs a clear, worthwhile objective** that players can actually perceive as worthwhile — not something only obvious in hindsight.
7. **Maintain pacing and variety.** Don't let an encounter run long or over-detailed for no payoff — if a single tavern conversation would deliver the same information as searching an entire village, use the tavern conversation.
8. **Calibrate challenge to party strength.** A party at roughly two-thirds of its expected strength should still be able to clear an encounter, but only with real effort; a full-strength party should clear it more comfortably. For a puzzle: **roughly two out of three tables should be able to solve it with effort** — if fewer than that are succeeding, the puzzle is miscalibrated, not the players. No single encounter should cost the party more than about 10% of its total health and healing resources.
9. **Mix roleplay, physical/teamwork challenges, and combat within every round.** Combat is the hardest of the three to run well and works best as connective tissue and as the climax, not as constant filler.
10. **The adventure should be finishable in the time allotted** by a competent table. Finishing is itself part of the reward; running out of time despite good play breeds frustration, not satisfaction.
11. **Don't overwrite.** No more than roughly a third of all encounters should be combat encounters, and the very first encounter of a session should never be one.
12. **Stay inside the game's own milieu.** Don't import mechanics or flavor that belong to a different setting or genre just because they seem neat.
13. **Avoid randomness in adventure structure; use logic to link encounters together.** Players feel genuinely satisfied when they can perceive the underlying logic connecting one part of the world to the next — a world that feels arbitrary breaks immersion even when individual encounters are well designed.
14. **Always allow alternate, unscripted solutions.** Provide at least one intended solution and a short list of approaches that won't work, but any solution that reasonably makes sense inside the fiction should have a real chance at success. The measure of success is whether players are *thinking*, not whether they land on the exact solution the designer had in mind. Never let a plot depend on one specific player action occurring, or on the survival of one specific NPC or item — players are too unpredictable for a plot to hinge on a single point of failure.
15. *(Restated from the handbook's broader guidance)* **Always telegraph danger before it lands.** Any puzzle, riddle, trap, or unusually deadly encounter needs sufficient warning and clues beforehand. The goal is a solvable challenge, not a "gotcha."
16. **Never force a decision on players mid-scene.** If the story needs a decision point, place it between scenes or before play begins — forcing it mid-round breeds frustration rather than tension.

### 2.4 The Encounter Taxonomy

The handbook catalogs distinct encounter *types*, useful as a design checklist to ensure variety across a session rather than defaulting to combat-and-conversation on repeat:

- **Negotiation/Interaction** — requires working with an NPC.
- **Deception** — the encounter is not what it first appears to be.
- **Dramatic Event** — an interlude delivering a clue, warning, or moral beat rather than a challenge to overcome.
- **Trick/Puzzle** — a non-combat challenge, generally with minimal potential for real harm.
- **Trap** — a non-combat hazard designed to inflict damage or delay, with a way to avoid or reduce the consequence.
- **Obstacle** — a natural hazard that must be physically crossed or overcome.
- **Dilemma** — a difficult, potentially dangerous situation with real risk if handled incorrectly.
- **Empty Room/Delay** — a deliberate pause with little to gain or lose.
- **Surprise/Ambush** — unavoidable, testing quick reaction rather than preparation.
- **Skirmish** — a short, low-lethality fight meant to drain resources rather than threaten the party.
- **Melee** — a significant combat encounter meant to meaningfully deplete party resources.
- **Guardian/Watchdog** — an unavoidable obstacle-monster blocking progress, resolvable through combat, negotiation, or cleverness.
- **Special Monster** — usually a single new or altered creature built around a specific unusual ability.
- **Finale** — the adventure's climax: a carefully built final combat with real tactical texture, surprises, and terrain or weaknesses the party can exploit.

### 2.5 Pacing Math

As a rule of thumb: roughly **one encounter per half hour of play**, with a full combat encounter counted as roughly 1.5 ordinary encounters for pacing purposes. A typical four-hour session, on this math, needs somewhere around six to eight substantive encounters, plus perhaps one to three low-stakes "uneventful" beats that a competent table will move past quickly.

### 2.6 Building NPCs and Player Characters

Important NPCs need documented motivations, habits, and pre-planned tactical responses written down in advance, specifically so the person running them at the table isn't improvising their entire personality cold, mid-session. Player character personality notes, similarly, should be concise (the handbook caps this at a single page), should encourage party unity while still allowing minor believable friction, and should never make a player character secretly evil or a traitor to the party — that role, again, belongs only to NPCs. If one player character gets an interesting personality quirk, every character should get one, so no single player has an unfair roleplaying advantage over the rest of the table.

### 2.7 Never Playtest Your Own Work

The handbook is blunt about this: an adventure's own author is the worst possible person to catch its flaws, because they already know how it's "supposed" to go. A different person needs to run it, observe quietly, and report back honestly — and that feedback should be received as a service, not an attack on the work.

### 2.8 The Dice-Witnessing Rule

Players roll their own attack rolls, damage, and saving throws — but **another player at the table witnesses each roll.** The person running the game rolls everything the players themselves shouldn't have advance knowledge of, such as a hidden ability check the character wouldn't know they'd succeeded or failed at. This is *peer-witnessed player dice*, not organizer-witnessed or specially calibrated dice — worth stating precisely, because those are two genuinely different practices, and only the peer-witness version is attested in this specific source.

### 2.9 Structured Mutual Feedback

The handbook includes standardized feedback forms used after a tournament round — one for players to evaluate their judge (the RPGA-era term for what AL now calls a DM), and a mirrored one for the judge to evaluate the team. Both use an identical five-point rating scale across six parallel categories, covering things like rules knowledge, handling the unexpected, roleplay quality, and overall performance. Both forms also ask participants to nominate standout performances in specific categories (rules knowledge, innovation, strategic play, leadership, roleplay, team support).

The principle worth carrying forward, independent of the exact forms: **the person running the table deserves the same structured, multi-category reflection the players are held to** — not vague post-session small talk, but a real accounting across more than just "was combat fun."

## CHAPTER 3 — THE WEST MARCHES MODEL

### 3.1 Origin

"West Marches" is a specific campaign style first described in 2007 by game designer Ben Robbins, writing about a home game he ran under that name. It was not created to be a generic "sandbox" label — it was Robbins' specific solution to a specific problem: he had roughly fifteen people who wanted to play, but getting any fixed subset of them together on a regular schedule was consistently difficult. The format he built solves that exact problem, and everything in this chapter should be understood as downstream of that original goal.

The term has since drifted in casual usage to mean something closer to "any game with a rotating cast and player-driven exploration," which is looser than what Robbins originally built. Where this document draws a distinction between the strict original model and looser modern usage, it says so explicitly.

### 3.2 The Three Load-Bearing Pillars

Robbins defined the format around three negations:

1. **No regular time.** Sessions are scheduled by the players themselves, whenever enough of them can actually gather — not on a fixed weekly slot.
2. **No regular party.** Each session draws its cast from a larger standing pool of available players (Robbins' own pool was roughly ten to fourteen people), rather than the same fixed group every time.
3. **No regular plot.** The players themselves decide where to go and what to do. There is no central quest-giver character steering them, and no single overarching storyline — just a persistent, consistent environment for them to explore on their own initiative.

### 3.3 Structural Elements Implied But Not Explicit in the Three Pillars

A number of practical structural details are necessary to make the three pillars actually work at a table, even though Robbins didn't spell them out as a fourth or fifth headline rule:

**A shared point of origin.** Every session begins and ends at the same home base (in Robbins' game, a specific tavern). This is what allows each session to be fully self-contained — characters never need to spontaneously materialize in narratively awkward ways, and as the distance between town and the frontier grows over a campaign's life, travel time itself becomes something the table increasingly abstracts away rather than plays out step by step.

**Destinations decided in advance.** Players tell the GM where they intend to go *before* the session, typically as part of arranging the GM's time in the first place. This lets the GM prepare a focused slice of content rather than needing the entire world simulated and ready at every moment.

**Session reports as a genuinely load-bearing shared resource, not an optional nicety.** Because every character is assumed to share the same home base between sessions, information travels through the whole player pool by default. Players are expected to write up and share what happened, specifically so that two different groups don't independently attempt to defeat the same named threat without knowing whether someone already succeeded. A new lead discovered by one group becomes available to any other group at the table.

**A shared, explicitly unreliable world map.** Discovered locations get marked on a communal map — but that map originates in-fiction from a character with imperfect mapmaking skill, and gets further edited (and occasionally mis-edited) by the players themselves over time. Getting lost because the map is simply wrong is treated as a built-in, intended possibility, correctable later by the players rather than something the GM prevents.

**Deliberately engineered competition between players.** A degree of jealousy over who gets to pick up an unresolved thread, or who found something exciting first, is treated as a genuine and intentional motivator for actually getting sessions scheduled — particularly when a valuable discovery is on the line.

**Danger tiered by distance from home**, letting players roughly self-assess risk without needing to be told explicitly — territory further from town is, on average, more dangerous. Pockets of unusually tough danger closer to home are deliberately well-signposted rather than hidden, both to keep the map feeling varied and to give stronger, returning characters a reason to revisit earlier territory.

### 3.4 Danger Was Designed, Not Incidental

Robbins is explicit that the lethality in his game was deliberate, not an accident of a harsh ruleset. Dice were rolled entirely in the open — never behind a screen, never fudged or glossed over — so that consequences were genuinely unyielding. The stated reasoning: if outcomes are real and consistent, players can make genuinely intelligent decisions that determine their own fate, rather than trusting that the GM will quietly soften the blow if things go badly. Danger, handled this way, becomes a force that pushes the whole table toward real teamwork and real caution, rather than a punishment mechanic.

This required the world itself to have consistent internal logic — an inconsistent world can't be "read" by players trying to make informed decisions about risk.

**Danger gradients as a concrete system:** Robbins tagged each region of his map with an explicit encounter-difficulty rating, rising the further a region sat from town. Adjacent regions were kept close in difficulty (no steep jumps), and "distance" was measured as actual walking distance through connected terrain, not straight-line distance — so a region on the far side of a mountain range could be considerably more dangerous than its straight-line distance from town would suggest, simply because it was effectively farther away in practice. Natural terrain features (mountains, rivers, valleys) split the map into distinct branching paths of exploration, and players tended to push progressively deeper along whichever path they'd already started, rather than jumping randomly between unrelated regions.

### 3.5 Practical Advice for Building and Running the Format

Drawn from Robbins' own later, more practically-oriented writing on the subject:

**Building it:**
- Make the physical distinction between town and wilderness sharp and legible — a walled town, or one bounded by a natural feature like a river, with strict order maintained inside it and effectively none enforced outside it.
- Avoid NPC adventurers almost entirely. If NPCs are already off adventuring on their own, it becomes harder to justify why players are the first to find anything, and it shifts narrative focus away from player-initiated action.
- Build dungeons and sites with pockets that resist being fully cleared — treasure rooms and locked areas that remain genuinely hard even for a strong party, so that the rare full clear becomes something worth bragging about at the table.

**Running it:**
- The GM should appear passive even while the world itself stays active. The framing that matters: it isn't the GM killing the party, it's the creature, the trap, the world itself. Rolling dice openly reinforces this.
- New players need an easy, low-effort hook to get them moving — something as simple as a vague treasure rumor is enough to get a fresh face out of town and into the game.
- Let the players themselves maintain the shared map and write the session summaries. Doing it for them, even with good intentions, trains them not to bother.

### 3.6 Named Social Failure Modes — and Why They Matter More Here Than in a Normal Game

Robbins is unusually candid that West Marches is, structurally, more socially fragile than an ordinary weekly game. In a normal campaign, a player has an established place in a fixed group — they don't have to wonder whether they belong. In this format, that security doesn't exist by default, and Robbins names this directly as the single biggest risk to the format's success.

Concrete countermeasures worth building in from the start:
- **Cliques forming naturally** — countered by a rule requiring parties to mix membership after some fixed number of sessions together.
- **Certain players being repeatedly excluded from follow-up trips** because faster-moving groups fill up first — countered, in the opposite direction, by a rule requiring a party that started an expedition together to stay together for a minimum number of sessions, which discourages purely opportunistic recruiting.
- **Scheduling requests must go through one shared, visible channel** (in Robbins' era, a mailing list; today, more likely a shared chat or scheduling tool) rather than being arranged privately, specifically so the game doesn't quietly splinter into several disconnected sub-games nobody else can see into.

### 3.7 The Historical Ancestor: Dave Arneson and Blackmoor

It is tempting to assume Dave Arneson — co-creator of Dungeons & Dragons and originator of the Blackmoor setting in the early 1970s — invented something equivalent to West Marches decades before Robbins named it. That's not quite accurate, and the precise distinction matters for how this doctrine is sourced.

Arneson never described or labeled what he was doing in West Marches terms, and his original goals were different: Blackmoor grew directly out of a wargaming hobbyist culture (the Castle & Crusade Society), where a shared fictional kingdom was divided into parcels among a loose community of players, rather than being a deliberate solution to the specific "getting busy adults to schedule a session" problem Robbins was solving.

That said, Arneson's own account of his campaign — recorded in his 1977 supplement *The First Fantasy Campaign* — describes something structurally deep and directly relevant: by the fourth year of continuous play, the Blackmoor setting had grown large enough to sustain **more than a hundred participants and a dozen separate referees running independently**, without Arneson's own continued involvement. That is documented **multi-GM, self-sustaining shared-world play** — arguably an even closer structural relative to a modern, multi-GM West Marches community than Robbins' original single-GM game is. Arneson's notes also candidly record a kind of generational drift within his own player base: newer players gravitating purely toward dungeon-crawling and losing interest in the overland, kingdom-management side of the game that had defined its early years — a useful, honestly-sourced data point about how a persistent shared world naturally evolves participant behavior over time, without anyone centrally directing that shift.

The accurate framing, then: **Arneson didn't originate or name West Marches, but he did originate its deeper structural ancestor** — a persistent world outliving and outscaling any single referee — nearly thirty-five years before Robbins put a name to a related, but distinct, format.

Separately, a well-regarded community discussion thread on the subject (an RPG StackExchange answer widely cited in the hobby) makes the same connection independently: it treats "Blackmoor" and "West Marches" as two related poles of a shared underlying genre — distinguished partly by how central player-versus-player conflict is to each — rather than treating them as unrelated. That's an independent confirmation of the same historical link, arrived at from a different angle.

## CHAPTER 4 — THE DEEPER RESERVOIR: FIFTY YEARS OF TABLE-CRAFT

Where the three layers above are silent on a specific point, this project draws on the accumulated, distilled judgment of the entire hobby's history — from the original 1970s wargaming-derived campaigns through every subsequent edition, up to the present. This is never a source of specific rules or mechanics to import wholesale; different editions have radically different math and radically different design assumptions, and importing those directly would be incoherent. It is, instead, a source of *judgment*: what has and hasn't actually worked at real tables, across nearly the entire lifespan of the hobby, regardless of which specific ruleset was in use at the time.

The Arneson material in Chapter 3 is the clearest anchor point for the early end of this reservoir. As more historical material is gathered, it accumulates here.

## CHAPTER 5 — THE DEEP GROUNDS FRAME

### 5.1 The Problem It Solves

Adventurers League tables, by design, have a structural churn problem: attendance rotates, party strength varies session to session, and players drop in and out unpredictably. Most tables simply tolerate this as an unavoidable inconvenience. This project instead treats it as something to actively absorb into the fiction itself, rather than something the fiction has to awkwardly work around.

### 5.2 The Company

**The Deep Grounds Adventuring Company** — motto "Delving Deep and Brewing Trouble" — is the in-fiction institution every player character operates under at this table. It is deliberately **institutional rather than a fixed roster.**

If a regular player steps away for a stretch of real time, their character was never "written out" — they were simply off handling other contracted Company business, off-page, exactly as a real adventuring company's members might rotate through different jobs. If a new player joins the table, their character is simply a new hire, or a one-job contractor picked up wherever the current module's opening scene happens to place them — a tavern, a guildhall, a waystation.

This framing absorbs AL's structural churn as ordinary, expected texture rather than a continuity problem requiring narrative gymnastics to explain away.

### 5.3 Canon Persistence

A further governing principle of this project: **what happens at this specific table is treated as the established canon antecedent for future remasters**, going forward. Where a future module needs to reference prior events, the Deep Grounds table's own history — not a generic, unstated assumption about "what usually happens" in the printed adventure — is what gets referenced.

This is precisely the same problem West Marches and Blackmoor both solve at the scale of an entire rotating player pool (Chapter 3): making a persistent world coherent despite an inconsistent cast. The Deep Grounds frame solves the same underlying problem at the scale of a single table with a rotating cast, rather than a whole community with a rotating cast and multiple GMs.

## CHAPTER 6 — THE APPLICATION PROCEDURE

When remastering a module for the Deep Grounds table, work through the following steps, in order, for every design decision:

**Step 1 — Check the AL compliance stack (Chapter 1).**
Does the Adaptation Guide, DM's Guide, Player's Guide, or the adventure's own text already speak to this specific point? If yes, that answer is final, applied literally. Stop here — do not consult any layer below.

**Step 2 — If AL is silent, check RPGA doctrine (Chapter 2).**
Does the Tournament Handbook's design philosophy, encounter taxonomy, or pacing math bear on this point — and does it actually fit a modern table (not a 1987 competitive-elimination tournament context)? If yes, and it genuinely fits, apply it.

**Step 3 — If RPGA is also silent, check West Marches doctrine (Chapter 3).**
Does this concern campaign-level structure — session flow, faction or world-state persistence, information propagation between sessions, how a rotating cast is absorbed — that Robbins' model or the broader community practice addresses? If yes, apply it, filtered through the Deep Grounds institutional frame (Chapter 5).

**Step 4 — If all three are silent, draw on the deeper historical reservoir (Chapter 4).**
Apply judgment informed by the accumulated table-craft wisdom of the hobby's full history, not any single edition's specific mechanics.

**Step 5 — A lower layer never overrides a higher one.**
If an instinct drawn from a lower layer would conflict with settled AL compliance, established RPGA framing, or established West Marches structure, the higher layer wins without exception.

**Step 6 — Document the reasoning inline.**
Every correction or design decision gets a visible note explaining the math and citing which layer justified it — not just stating that a change was made, but showing why.

or ambiguous. Chapter 6, Step 6 requires that every design decision carry a visible note citing the
layer that justified it. A numbered ruling is that note, made durable.

### 7.2 — SR-8 · The Menagerie keeps only creatures without language

**The gap.** The 2024 DMG's Menagerie says *"typically only Beasts and some Monstrosities can be
kept… though the DM might allow other creatures."* It never says which Monstrosities. AL is silent.
Step 4 applies.

**The ruling.** A creature with `Languages: None` may be kept. A creature that speaks — **or that
understands speech and cannot answer** — may not. Housing it is imprisonment, not husbandry.

**Why Languages and not Intelligence.** Both are in the SRD; they disagree on eight creatures, and
Languages is right on all eight. An INT ≤ 3 line excludes the Ape (INT 6), the Raven (5) and the
Baboon (4) — which is what a menagerie is *for*. And it keeps the Giant Vulture, whose entry reads
*"Understands Common but can't speak."* **A creature that knows it is imprisoned is a prisoner.**
The number could not reach that; the ruling could.

**Effect.** 89 of the SRD's Beast/Monstrosity pool at CR ≤ 3 are keepable. Excluded: Doppelganger,
Manticore, Minotaur of Baphomet, Werewolf, Winter Wolf, Merrow, Wererat, Harpy, Giant Vulture.

### 7.3 — SR-9 · A cunning monstrosity may take its keeper

**The ruling.** `Monstrosity · Languages: None · INT ≥ 4` — Ettercap, Mimic, Phase Spider — are
**never Bastion Defenders**. On an attack rolled on the Menagerie that is violent and lethal, each
rolls 1d6; a 1 hits. Targets in strict order: other creatures in the room, then the handlers in that
room, then a hireling elsewhere at random — and reaching that third stage is a **breach**. Only the
creatures that rolled a 1 leave; the rest were docile. The fed stay caged; the door between the
Menagerie and the house is shut. In an empty house they still go, but the keep is under attack at
that instant, so on the way out they may defend it once, by accident.

**Why the type split is not arbitrary.** Beast + INT ≥ 4 is Ape, Baboon, Raven, Giant Octopus,
Giant Weasel — clever animals. Monstrosity + INT ≥ 4 is a predator with a plan. The chapter's own
taxonomy does the work: Beast means natural, Monstrosity means it should not exist. **A raven is
smart. It is not plotting.**

**The rule needs no "don't."** Four cunning creatures is a 1.6% breach per attack; sixteen — one
full room, since Ettercap is Medium — is 51.3%. The curve says it. And prey animals are armour,
because they are eaten first.

### 7.4 — SR-10 · Non-defenders harvest

**The ruling.** A creature that cannot defend — too dangerous (SR-9) or too small to fight —
produces instead. **10 GP per ¼ CR, per head, per turn**, using the chapter's own CR brackets.

**Derived, not authored.** The chapter's price ladder is already linear in CR, so a flat rate per
¼ CR gives a flat ~25-turn payback at every CR. One number, no table, and it cannot drift: reprice a
creature and the yield moves with it. A percentage-of-cost rate was considered and **rejected** — it
multiplies two things that both scale with CR, collapsing payback from 100 turns at CR ⅛ to 4.2 at
CR 3. Everyone buys phase spiders and the room becomes one creature deep.

**Brackets, not raw CR**, because 15 of 18 Tiny creatures are CR 0 and strict CR would silently
delete this ruling for them. The chapter itself refuses to distinguish (*"0 or ⅛ → 50 GP"*).

**The symmetry is the point.** Too dangerous to trust at the wall, or too small to matter at it —
both ends resolve to one rule, and nothing in the middle needs a special case.

### 7.5 — SR-11 · Menagerie creatures count on top of the Barrack's cap

The chapter says creatures in a Menagerie **are** Bastion Defenders. It does not say they are
barracked. `BASTION_BARRACKS_CAP` (12 roomy / 25 vast) governs the Barrack; a Menagerie is a
different room. Step 1: AL is silent, the chapter is silent, the silence is genuine.

### 7.6 — SR-12 · Eldritch Discovery bestows a talisman, not a Charm

**The gap.** The Observatory's Empower option bestows a Charm on *"you or another creature of your
choice that is on the same plane of existence."* A Bastion turn is resolved by one player, alone,
between sessions. **There is no other creature present.** And AL already speaks to cross-character
Bastion benefit, pointing the other way: *characters within the same session may combine Bastions
and use resolved benefits in the session they play together.*

**The ruling.** The Charm arrives as a **physical talisman**: a common magic item, `consumable`,
`UNTRADEABLE`, holding one of Darkvision, Heroism or Vitality. The chapter's unnamed die is a coin
flip. The talisman counts against **both** the charm gift cap (2/5/5/5) and the consumable carry
pool (5/10/10/15) — the same pool as scrolls and potions, so each one costs a potion slot.

**Why this resolves it.** "Another creature of your choice" stops being an abstraction resolved from
a kitchen on a Tuesday and becomes *a thing you hand somebody at a table*. **The handoff itself is
out of scope** — this platform handles everything around the table, not the table. Both ends are
already served: the giver files a DM-approved disposal, the recipient logs a DM-vouched item.

*No such item exists in SRD 5.2 (2 common magic items: Bead of Nourishment, Potion of Climbing) or
in the DMG's common tables (Dark Shard Amulet, Clockwork Amulet — both with their own effects). The
talisman is the Exchange's own.*

### 7.7 — SR-13 · A named craft option governs a tool-derived one

**The ruling.** Every facility with tools makes anything those tools can make. Where a tool-derived
item is **also** a named option in the chapter, **the named option governs.**

**Why it is load-bearing.** Without it the Scriptorium derives `Spell Scroll` from Calligrapher's
Supplies with no class list and no level cap — because the SRD's tool line says only "Spell Scroll"
— and the chapter's constrained option (Cleric/Wizard, 3rd or lower) becomes bypassable. Two paths
to one thing, never compared: the shape of every serious bug found on 17 July. With it, the
Scriptorium derives `{Ink, Spell Scroll}`, the scroll collapses into the constrained row, and Ink
arrives for free.

**This is a Step 1 ruling, not a Step 4 one** — the chapter states both the derivation and the
constraint. It is silent only on their precedence, and only one precedence leaves both true.
